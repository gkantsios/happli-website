A deposit reduces no-shows because the client has already paid something to hold the slot. Skipping now costs them money, so they show up, or they cancel early enough for you to rebook the time. The deposit works best when it's taken at booking, sized to the service, backed by a short written policy, and repeated in every reminder. Booking software with deposits does the collecting. The policy is what changes behavior.

Here's how to set that up, step by step, for a salon, spa, lash or brow studio, esthetician room or spray tan studio.

## Step 1: Write your policy before you turn deposits on

Decide the rules first, then put them in your booking settings. A policy answers four questions:

- How much is the deposit?
- How late can a client cancel or reschedule and keep it?
- What happens to it if they cancel late or don't show?
- Does it come off the final bill?

**Why it helps:** clients push back on rules they didn't see coming. They rarely argue with a rule they read and agreed to before they paid. A clear policy also saves you from deciding case by case at 9 p.m. on a Friday.

**Example:** a lash studio writes: "A deposit holds your appointment and comes off your total. Cancel or reschedule at least 48 hours ahead and we'll move it to your new time. Inside 48 hours, or if you don't show, the deposit is kept."

Put the policy on your website, on your booking page and in your confirmation message. Three places is not too many.

## Step 2: Decide which appointments need a deposit

You don't have to take a deposit on everything. Start where a no-show hurts most.

- **Long appointments.** A full lash set, a color correction or a facial with add-ons blocks hours you can't easily fill on short notice.
- **New clients.** You have no history with them yet.
- **Peak times.** Saturday mornings, evenings, and the weeks before holidays, proms and weddings.
- **Prep-heavy services.** Anything where you mix product, prep a room or book an assistant in advance.

**Why it helps:** a deposit on a 15-minute brow wax can feel like more hassle than it's worth to a regular. A deposit on a three-hour set protects real money.

**Example:** a spa takes deposits on every online booking, since most of those are new clients, but lets the front desk skip it for regulars booking their next visit in person.

## Step 3: Pick an amount that fits the service

A percentage of the service price is the simplest rule, because the deposit grows with the time you're holding. A flat amount is easy to explain but feels heavy on a small service and light on a big one.

**Why it helps:** the deposit has to be big enough that skipping stings a little, and small enough that it doesn't feel like paying in full a week early.

**Example:** at 25%, a $60 brow lamination needs a $15 deposit and a $200 lash set needs $50. Some studios go lower to keep booking easy, and some go higher for their longest services. There's no single right number. Pick one, run it for a couple of months, and look at what changed.

## Step 4: Use booking software with deposits so clients pay as they book

The strongest setup is online booking where the client pays the deposit to finish booking. No deposit, no appointment.

**Why it helps:** when the deposit is a separate step ("we'll text you a link"), some clients never pay it, and now you're chasing money for a slot that's already on your calendar. Taking it as part of booking removes that gap. It also means a stranger who finds you at 11 p.m. can book and commit in one visit.

**Example:** a client picks a gel manicure on your booking page, sees the deposit and your policy on the last step, pays, and gets a confirmation. If they back out before paying, nothing is booked and the slot stays open.

## Step 5: Handle front-desk and phone bookings the same way

Not every booking comes in online. Calls, DMs and walk-ins who book their next visit at checkout all count.

**Why it helps:** if online clients pay a deposit and phone clients don't, your no-shows move to the phone. Clients notice, too.

**Example:** when a client calls for a Saturday color appointment, the front desk books it and collects the deposit right then, by card or with a payment link, before the call ends. If your software lets you require deposits for bookings made by your team as well as online, turn that on so nobody forgets.

## Step 6: Send reminders that repeat the policy

Deposits and reminders work together. The deposit gives the client a reason to come. The reminder makes sure they remember.

**Why it helps:** most no-shows aren't people trying to skip. They forgot, or they meant to cancel and didn't get around to it. A reminder sent before your cancellation window closes gives them a last easy chance to move the appointment while you can still fill it.

**Example:** with a 48-hour window, send a reminder about three days out. "Hi Maya, see you Saturday at 10 a.m. for your lash fill. Need to move it? Reschedule by Thursday at 10 a.m. to keep your deposit." Then send a short day-before reminder with the address and any prep notes.

## Step 7: Know what you'll do when someone still doesn't show

Deposits cut no-shows. They don't end them. Decide the routine now:

1. **Mark the appointment as a no-show** in your booking software, so it's in the client's history and your reports.
2. **Keep the deposit**, as your policy says.
3. **Charge a no-show fee** if your policy has one and the deposit doesn't cover the loss.
4. **Send one calm message.** "We missed you today. Per our policy the deposit is kept. Here's a link to rebook when you're ready."
5. **Watch for repeats.** A client with two no-shows might be asked to pay in full up front next time.

**Why it helps:** handling every no-show the same way keeps it from feeling personal, for you and for the client. It also means you're not inventing a response while you're annoyed.

**Example:** a stylist has a client miss a 2-hour balayage. The deposit is kept, the no-show is logged, and the client gets one friendly text with a rebooking link. No back-and-forth.

## A deposit and cancellation policy you can copy

Use this as a starting point. Change the amounts and times to fit your studio, and check it against your local rules. It isn't legal advice, so have it reviewed if you're unsure.

> **Deposits and cancellations**
>
> A deposit of [X]% of your service is required to book. It holds your time and comes off your total at checkout.
>
> **Need to cancel or reschedule?** Let us know at least [48] hours before your appointment and we'll move your deposit to your new time [or refund it].
>
> **Late cancellations and no-shows.** If you cancel or reschedule with less than [48] hours' notice, or don't arrive for your appointment, your deposit is kept. (Optional: a no-show fee of [X]% of the service may also be charged.)
>
> **Running late?** We'll do our best to fit you in. If you're more than [15] minutes late, we may need to shorten or reschedule your service, and the deposit rules above apply.
>
> By booking, you agree to this policy. Thank you for understanding. It lets us keep our prices fair and our schedule open for everyone.

## Common mistakes

- **Hiding the policy.** If the first time a client sees the rule is when you keep their money, expect a fight and maybe a bad review.
- **Making exceptions for some clients and not others.** Waive a deposit once and it becomes the expectation. If you make exceptions, make them by rule.
- **Taking the deposit after booking.** A "pay later" link leaves the slot booked and the deposit unpaid. Collect it as part of booking.
- **Setting a cancellation window you won't enforce.** A 48-hour window you never hold to teaches clients it doesn't matter.
- **Forgetting to take the deposit off the bill.** Clients remember being charged twice. Check that checkout subtracts it.
